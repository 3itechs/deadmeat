01

What We Found on the Site Today

We went through the homepage and the Dead Skull Spatula page on desktop and mobile before writing a word of this. Twelve findings, numbered for reference. Nothing here is theoretical — every item is something we observed on the live store.

F‑01

The hero sells a feeling, not a product

“A Movement for Grilling Enthusiasts” is brand poetry above a motion-blurred video. A first-time visitor from an ad sees no product, no price, no offer, and no reason to act. The headline also breaks mid-word — “ENTHUSIAST / S” wraps onto two lines at desktop width, which reads as a broken page.

Fix › Hero must answer four things in one screen: what it is, why it’s better, what it costs, what to do next. Brand line moves to a band underneath.

Critical
F‑02

Typo in the welcome popup — the first thing every visitor sees

The entry popup reads “YOU’VE GOT AN EXLCUSIVE WELCOME KIT.” It is the very first impression, it sits over a $59.99 offer, and for a brand charging premium prices for a spatula, a misspelling at the door quietly costs trust before anyone reaches a product.

Fix › Same-day correction. Costs nothing, protects everything downstream.

Critical
F‑03

Mobile: two overlays cover the product

On a 375px phone, the sticky “Exclusive Welcome Offer” rail runs down the left edge over the image thumbnails and the product title, while the chat bubble sits on the right and clips the charity line to “donated to cha…”. Most Shopify traffic is mobile, so this is the version that actually matters.

Fix › Collapse the offer rail to a dismissible bottom bar, delay chat until scroll depth >50%, and keep a clear channel around price and Add to Cart.

High
F‑04

Wrong reviews on the wrong product

The Dead Skull Spatula page shows reviews that are plainly about the tongs — “Tongs that grip, lift and look great… love the lock”. A shopper comparing tools reads this as padded or careless social proof, exactly where they are deciding whether to trust a $54.99 price.

Fix › Filter the review widget to the product, with a separate “All Society reviews” tab below it.

High
F‑05

21 reviews carrying a premium price

4.95 out of 5 is excellent. Twenty-one reviews is thin for a tool priced 4–5× the supermarket alternative. Review volume, not just rating, is what makes a high price feel safe.

Fix › Post-purchase review flow with photo incentive; pull the strongest existing lines into the page as designed pull-quotes rather than leaving them in the widget.

High
F‑06

The free-shipping threshold is invisible — and the mid bundle misses it

Free shipping starts at $150. The hero spatula is $54.99, leaving a $95.01 gap that is never shown to the shopper. Worse, the Dead Grill Station Kit at $119.99 lands $30.01 short of the threshold — the bundle built to raise order value doesn’t clear the bar built to raise order value.

Fix › Add a cart progress bar (“$30.01 to free shipping”) and either reprice that kit to $149.99 or drop the threshold to $120. This is the single fastest AOV win on the store.

High
F‑07

The bundle discount is too shallow to drive the trade-up

The OG DMS Toolkit is $249.99 against $279.94 — a 10.7% saving. Shoppers are trained to expect 15–25% for committing to a kit. At 10.7% the bundle reads as a rounding error rather than a deal, so the AOV lift it was designed for doesn’t fire.

Fix › Restructure to a tiered kit builder where the saving visibly grows with each tool added, and anchor the à-la-carte total right beside it.

Medium
F‑08

Scroll-jacked hero delays every product

The opening section pins and plays through on scroll — eight full wheel scrolls barely advanced the page. Paid traffic lands with intent and a few seconds of patience; making them scrub through a cinematic before seeing a product bleeds sessions.

Fix › Cap the pinned sequence, or drop it entirely on paid landing pages. Products within one screen of the fold.

Medium
F‑09

“Limited to 500” is real scarcity, used as a footnote

The OG Toolkit is genuinely capped at 500 units — that is honest, verifiable scarcity, which is rare and valuable. Right now it lives inside a long product title where nobody reads it.

Fix › Surface a live “X of 500 claimed” counter on the bundle page. Only ever show a real number.

Medium
F‑10

Red means four different things

The brand red currently carries the logo, the sale badges, the offer rail and the Add to Cart button. When the action colour is also the decoration colour, the eye stops treating it as “click here” and the primary CTA loses its pull on a black page.

Fix › Reserve one exact red for actions only. Badges and brand marks move to bone, char, or a second accent.

Medium
F‑11

No reassurance where the money decision happens

Shipping terms, returns and secure-checkout copy all exist — at the very bottom of the product page. The doubts they answer occur at the price, not after the reviews.

Fix › Compact trust row directly under Add to Cart: returns window, secure checkout, ships-by date, guarantee.

Medium
F‑12

The charity story is the differentiator and it’s set in body text

10% of net profits donated is the one thing no competitor can copy overnight, and it converts — particularly for gift buyers, who need permission to spend $55 on a spatula. It currently appears as a single line of small type.

Fix › Make it a designed above-fold component with the partner logos, and give it a named block in the component library so every landing page inherits it.

Upside
02

The Strategic Read

Before any design work, we need agreement on what is actually being bought. The reviews on your own site answer it more clearly than any brief could.

Nobody needs a $54.99 spatula. They want to be the guy who owns one.

The site currently sells community in the abstract — a movement, a tribe, a set of commandments. Your customers describe something much more concrete and much easier to sell: the moment a guest picks up the tool and asks where they got it. That is a status purchase wearing a utility purchase’s clothes, and it should be the spine of every landing page we build.

The commandments, the Society, and the charity commitment are not the pitch — they are the justification that makes the status purchase feel earned. Lead with the cookout. Close with the Society.

Typography that survives mobile

  • Locked type scale with defined minimums — no more headlines breaking mid-word (F-01).
  • Condensed display face for headlines, capped at a width that never orphans a letter.
  • Body copy at a minimum 16px with a 4.5:1 contrast floor on the dark ground.
  • Numerals set to tabular so prices, savings and counters align in every table and cart.

Photography direction

  • Retire the motion-blurred hero footage — movement reads as an unfinished page, not energy.
  • Every hero shot shows the tool in hand, at the grill, with fire, not floating on black.
  • One shot per page of the social moment: people around the cookout, not just product.
  • Product cutouts stay on char for consistency across the grid.

New proof components

  • Star rating and review count beside price, not below the fold.
  • Trust row under Add to Cart (F-11).
  • Free-shipping progress bar (F-06).
  • Charity block with partner logos (F-12).
  • Honest scarcity counter for capped drops (F-09).

How We Build It

We design in the browser, not in Figma. You approve a real, working page on a private link — on your own phone, at your own thumb speed — and that approved page becomes the Shopify build. The design you sign off on is the thing that ships, because it is literally the same file.

1 · Designed as working HTML

Every block is built as real, responsive HTML with proper colour and type tokens — the component library the whole programme is assembled from. The page you review is not a picture of a page.

2 · Reviewed on a private link

Each page goes to a staging URL you can open anywhere and forward to anyone. You see the real type rendering, the real responsive behaviour, and the real sticky Add to Cart under your own thumb — then approve or mark it up.

3 · Converted to Shopify sections

Once approved, the same markup and CSS become native Liquid sections with schema settings. No page-builder app, no monthly fee, no third-party script — and it stays in your theme if we ever part ways.

4 · You keep control

Every section exposes settings, so your team edits headlines, images, prices and offers directly in the Shopify theme editor. New page, new test, new season — no developer needed for copy changes.

Why not Figma? A Figma file is a drawing of a website. It cannot show you how the headline actually wraps on a 375px phone, how the type really renders, or how the sticky Add to Cart feels under a thumb — and those are exactly the things that were wrong on the current site. Designing in the browser also removes an entire translation step: there is no “the build does not match the design,” because the design is the build. On a two-week sprint, that step is days we do not have. The trade-off, stated honestly: exploring ten wildly different directions is faster in Figma than in code. So we explore loosely and cheaply up front, then commit early and refine in the browser — where refinement is faster. If you later need design assets beyond the web — packaging, print, an ad-creative library — that is a separate Figma exercise, and we should scope it separately rather than pay for it here.
On Replo and the page builders: they are genuinely good for non-technical teams shipping one-off pages, and if speed to a first page is the only priority we can start there. But they add a monthly cost and a third-party script, and pages built in them do not survive leaving the app. Hand-written Liquid also outperforms builder-generated markup on Core Web Vitals — and on mobile that difference is conversion, not vanity. We hold a performance budget on every page we ship.

The Testing Plan

Two heats, run at the same time. Some fixes are worth shipping today with no test at all — nobody needs an experiment to justify correcting a typo. The rest is a structured programme.

450°F

Hot & Fast

Shipped on day one of the sprint. High certainty, no test required — these are defects, not hypotheses.

  1. Fix the popup typo — F-02. Minutes of work.
  2. Clear the mobile overlays off the product title and CTA — F-03.
  3. Filter reviews to the product — F-04.
  4. Add the free-shipping progress bar and reprice the $119.99 kit over the threshold — F-06.
  5. Fix the headline wrap so no word breaks — F-01.
  6. Trust row under Add to Cart — F-11.
225°F

Low & Slow

Begins once the six pages are live. One variable per test, two-week cycles, winners scaled.

  1. Angle test — status/cookout vs. movement/community. The biggest swing we have.
  2. Format test — advertorial (LP-01) vs. direct-response (LP-02) on the same cold traffic.
  3. Offer test — kit discount depth, once LP-03 is live.
  4. Proof placement — reviews above vs. below the fold.
  5. Capture test — quiz (LP-05) vs. flat welcome discount.
  6. Seasonal — LP-04 live six weeks before each gifting peak.
An honest word on statistics. Proper A/B testing needs roughly 1,000+ sessions per variant before a result means anything. Unless the store is already at that volume per page, we run sequential tests on big swings rather than parallel splits on small ones, and we judge on add-to-cart rate and revenue per session — which move earlier and more visibly than conversion rate. We will confirm the real numbers in week one from your analytics and set the cadence to match. We would rather tell you a test is unreadable than hand you a false winner.
